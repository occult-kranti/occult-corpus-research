# Internet research and reproducibility findings

## Association of Internet Researchers

Source: AoIR Ethics page, https://aoir.org/ethics/ and *Internet Research: Ethical Guidelines 3.0*, https://aoir.org/reports/ethics3.pdf

AoIR presents its ethics reports as starting points for reflection rather than a universal checklist. The protocol should therefore be contextual: assess expectations of privacy, vulnerability, identifiability, likely harms, researcher safety, consent feasibility, data minimization, quotation risk, and the effects of publication. Public accessibility does not automatically eliminate ethical duties.

## Web scraping for research

Source: Brown et al., “Web scraping for research: Legal, ethical, institutional, and scientific considerations,” Big Data & Society, 2025, https://doi.org/10.1177/20539517251381686

The paper distinguishes traditional HTML scraping, undocumented API use, and browser-plugin collection, emphasizing that each has distinct legal, ethical, institutional, and scientific implications. Its checklist-oriented framing supports documenting access method, terms and robots signals, storage/sharing decisions, institutional review, security, and scientific validity. For this project, the browser extension’s authenticated, session-based collection must be described as access-limited and not equivalent to a public census.

## Computational reproducibility

Source: Piccolo and Frampton, “Tools and techniques for computational reproducibility,” GigaScience, 2016, https://doi.org/10.1186/s13742-016-0135-4

Reproducibility requires more than code: record operating system, dependency versions, software versions, parameters, preprocessing, execution order, and data provenance. Narrative documentation remains necessary even when scripts are automated. The handoff should therefore include frozen schemas, manifests, environment information, deterministic smoke tests, analysis parameters, and a changelog of deviations.
